What's happening now

4 ways education is changing

01

Proof of skills is starting to count as much as a diploma

Employers have opened almost 600,000 jobs to workers without a four-year degree, and 33 states have dropped degree requirements for many government jobs. But a Harvard study found 45% of companies that dropped the requirement changed only the job post, not who they hired. People who can show what they've done have the edge.

Source: Opportunity@Work (2026); Harvard Business School and Burning Glass Institute (2024)

What you can do
  • Keep a simple record of projects you finish and what they achieved.
  • Earn a short certificate in a skill employers are asking for.
  • See where the skills-first jobs are in the section below.
02

Everyone is getting a personal AI tutor

Elon Musk posted that "it's AI tutors or the slow death of civilization," and in 2026 his company xAI began rolling out its Grok chatbot as a nationwide tutor in El Salvador. But a two-year trial in 18 Tennessee middle schools found most students stopped using the Khanmigo AI tutor once it refused to just give answers. AI tutors help when people actually use them to learn.

Source: Mother Jones (Musk); FutureEd and NBER (Khanmigo, 2026)

What you can do
  • Use AI to explain, quiz and coach you, not to do the work for you (copy the prompts on this page).
  • Choose kids' tools that give hints and questions, not answers, and check their age rules.
  • Sit with younger kids for the first sessions.
  • Keep real teachers, classmates and study partners in the picture. AI can't replace them.
03

Learning doesn't stop at graduation anymore

That 39% figure at the top of the page means a degree earned 10 or 20 years ago won't carry anyone through a whole career anymore. AI, data and technology skills are growing fastest. Learning is becoming something adults do all their lives, not just in school.

Source: World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025

What you can do
  • Plan to learn one new work skill every year.
  • Set aside an hour a week for learning, like you would for exercise.
  • Ask your employer what training or tuition help it offers. Many companies pay for it.
  • Teach your kids that learning doesn't end at graduation, by letting them see you do it.
04

School is moving out of the classroom

Homeschooling grew an average of 5.4% across reporting states in 2024–25, and homeschoolers are now about 6% of U.S. students, up from about 3% before the pandemic. In 18 states, education savings accounts let some families use public education money on tutoring, classes or materials. Starting January 1, 2027, a new federal Education Freedom Tax Credit will fund K–12 scholarships through donations, but only in states whose governors opt in. Supporters call it the biggest expansion of school choice yet; groups including the National Education Association oppose it as a voucher program.

Source: Johns Hopkins Homeschool Hub via Reason (2025); EdChoice (2026); U.S. Treasury (2026)

What you can do
  • Look up your state's homeschool and education savings account rules on official state websites.
  • Check whether your state has opted in to the federal Education Freedom Tax Credit before 2027 (Treasury announcement).
  • Ask about free public virtual schools if you want structure without a classroom.
  • Find a co-op or group class so neither you nor your child learns alone.

Prompt: make AI your tutor

Personal tutor prompt

Teach me [topic] as if I'm a beginner. My goal is [why I want to learn it]. Explain one small step at a time with an everyday example. After each step, ask me one question to check I understood. If I get it wrong, explain it a different way. Don't move on until I get it right.

How to spot an employer that really hires for skills

A Harvard and Burning Glass Institute study found that 45% of companies that dropped degree requirements changed the job post but not who they actually hired. Look for these signs:

  • The post says "or equivalent experience" and lists skills, not just schools.
  • They test skills with a work sample, skills test or trial project.
  • They run apprenticeships or paid training programs.
  • People in that job don't all have degrees. Check current employees' profiles on LinkedIn.

Source: Harvard Business School and Burning Glass Institute (2024)

For your kids

How children will learn

Kids growing up now will use AI their whole lives. The goal isn't to keep them away from it. It's to help them think, solve problems and work with people, with AI as a helper. Here are projects to try at home, and how to use AI as their coach.

Learn by doing

Teach the problem, not the tools

When Elon Musk started his own school, Ad Astra, he used a simple idea: don't start with lessons on screwdrivers and wrenches. Give kids an engine and let them take it apart, and they'll see why the tools matter. His school also grouped kids by ability, not age. You can do both at home.

Ages 6+ · science

Take apart an old gadget

An old toaster, keyboard or remote (unplugged, batteries out, adult nearby). Guess what each part does, then look it up.

They learn: how machines work, using tools safely, asking good questions.

Ages 8+ · math

Plan the family's groceries

Give them a real budget and a week of meals to plan. Compare prices, add it up, and see if it fits.

They learn: math, money, trade-offs and planning.

Ages 9+ · technology

Build a simple game or website

Pick something they love and make a page or game about it with a free beginner tool like Scratch.

They learn: logic, design, and fixing what breaks.

Ages 10+ · business

Run a tiny business

A bake sale, pet-sitting or yard work. Set a price, track costs and count the profit.

They learn: math, people skills and responsibility.

Ages 10+ · research

Investigate a real question

"Why is our electric bill higher in summer?" Gather clues, test ideas and present what they found to the family.

They learn: research, reading, and checking whether a source is right.

Any age · life skills

Fix something together

A wobbly chair, a leaky faucet, a flat bike tire. Watch a how-to video, then do it.

They learn: patience, following steps, and that they can fix things.

Grades K–12 · AI

Enter the Presidential AI Challenge

A national contest where student teams, with an adult mentor, build an AI project that solves a problem in their community. Registration for 2026 has closed; watch for the next round.

They learn: AI basics, teamwork and solving real problems. Challenge website →

Use AI as the coach, not the answer key

Paste this into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini before your child starts a project, so the AI asks questions instead of doing the work.

Coach prompt for kids

You are a patient tutor for a [age]-year-old who is working on [project]. Do not give the answer or do the work. Ask one question at a time to help them figure it out. Give a small hint only if they are stuck after two tries. Use simple words, and cheer them on when they get something right.

Stay safe: check each AI tool's age rules, and sit with younger kids while they use it.

Learn at their level, not their grade

A 9-year-old might read like a 12-year-old and do math like an 8-year-old. That's normal. Let them move ahead where they're strong and slow down where they need it.

Practice tools like IXL adjust to your child's level automatically, and live classes on Outschool are grouped by interest and ability.

Don't skip the people part

Projects are better with others. Co-ops, library programs, sports and group classes give kids the teamwork and friendships that no AI can.
